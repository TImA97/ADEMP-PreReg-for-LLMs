		\subsection*{General Information}
		This template treats the repeated generation of ratings from one or more large language models (LLMs) as a simulation study \parencite[for details, see][]{angelikeEvaluatingLargeLanguage2026}. It adapts the ADEMP-PreReg template for Monte Carlo simulation studies \parencite{siepeSimulationStudiesMethodological2024}.  A Word version is available at \url{https://github.com/TImA97/ADEMP-PreReg-for-LLMs}. The \LaTeX{} version provides simple commands to exclude the instructions, explanations, and example boxes from the compiled document. To preregister and time-stamp your protocol, we recommend uploading it to the Open Science Framework (\url{https://osf.io/}) or Zenodo (\url{https://zenodo.org/}). When using this template, please cite the associated articles \parencite{siepeSimulationStudiesMethodological2024, angelikeEvaluatingLargeLanguage2026}. If you have any questions or suggestions for improving the template, please contact us via the ways described at \url{https://github.com/TImA97/ADEMP-PreReg-for-LLMs}. \\
		
		\subsection*{Using this template} 
		This template can be used for any study that uses LLM prompting to annotate, classify, or rate text materials (e.g., regarding psychological, semantic, or syntactic properties). Such applications involve many degrees of freedom: different task instructions, prompt details, and model versions may lead to different LLM output, and even repeated runs of an identical prompt may yield different results. The template therefore evaluates the quality of LLM ratings as a function of these (often arbitrary) decisions concerning prompt design, model choice, and implementation (e.g., whether ratings are taken from the generated text or derived from the probability distribution over response-option tokens). It can be adapted if ratings are obtained using other natural language processing methods (e.g., semantic vector embeddings, fine-tuned encoder classifiers).
		
		Please provide detailed answers to each of the questions. If you plan to perform multiple LLM simulation studies within the same project, you can either register them separately or number your answers to each question with an indicator for each study. As the planning and execution of LLM simulation studies often involves considerable complexity and unknowns, it may be difficult to answer all the questions in this template or some changes may be made along the analysis pathway. This is to be expected and should not deter researchers from planning or preregistering an LLM simulation study. If you preregistered your LLM simulation, any modifications to the protocol should simply be reported transparently along with justification, which will ultimately add credibility to your research.

	\section{Aims}
	\subsection{What is the aim of the LLM simulation study?}
	
	\explanation{The aim of an LLM simulation study refers to the goal of the research and shapes subsequent choices. Aims are typically related to evaluating the capability of different LLMs to fulfill a particular task like generating ratings for word lists. Depending on whether an external validation criterion is available to evaluate the LLM output, one can pursue one or both of two types of aims: 
		
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Investigating variance components in LLM-generated ratings to distinguish: (1)~\textit{construct variance} reflecting stimulus differences, (2) \textit{method variance} introduced by factors such as model choice or prompt design, and (3) \textit{residual variance} due to repetitions of LLM calls (i.e., stochastic error). Potential aims include a variance decomposition of LLM ratings regarding different models and prompting approaches, or the assessment of inter-rater reliability between different LLMs or between the stochastic, repeated output of the same LLM. Note that high consistency does not imply correct or valid ratings; LLMs may agree while being systematically biased.
			\item Investigating the correspondence of ratings obtained from one or more LLMs with an external gold standard, e.g., human ratings for the same task. This aim is most closely related to the traditional idea of simulation studies where a statistical method is evaluated with respect to its performance in recovering known, true parameters. Depending on whether the target is subjective (e.g., human norms) or objective (e.g., factual truth), relative or absolute agreement may be of interest.
	\end{enumerate}} 
	
	\begin{examplebox}
		The aims of the present study are (1) to conduct a variance decomposition of LLM ratings of valence and arousal regarding multi-word expressions into construct variance (due to differences in the valence and arousal of the included expressions), method variance (due to specific LLM and prompt variations), as well as residual variance (due to repeated LLM calls), and (2) to assess the extent to which LLM ratings correspond to the perceived valence and arousal of multi-word expressions as rated by humans. 
	\end{examplebox}

	\section{Performance Measures}
	\subsection{Which performance measures will be used?}    
	
	\explanation{Please state why specific performance measures are used and how they will be calculated. You may provide formulas to avoid ambiguity. The choice for specific performance measures strongly depends on the aim of the investigation. If the aim is to analyze the proportion of variance in LLM ratings due to different LLM model versions or prompt instructions, it may be necessary to specify a suitable mixed-effects model for the variance decomposition (e.g., using generalizability theory; \cite{brennanGeneralizabilityTheory2001}). If an external criterion is available against which LLM output is compared (e.g., human ratings), different measures of inter-rater reliability or agreement between human and LLM ratings can be computed (e.g., the correlation of ratings across all stimulus materials). In some scenarios, it may also be of interest to compare LLM output to an established gold standard using the bias or RMSE (e.g., when assessing whether LLMs can accurately predict a certain outcome of interest).}  
	
	\begin{examplebox}
		The present investigation has two aims:
		
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item Estimating variance components in LLM valence and arousal explained by differences in the stimulus materials and differences in methods (secondary performance measure) using generalizability theory \parencite{brennanGeneralizabilityTheory2001}.
			\item Assessing the correlation between human and LLM ratings of valence and arousal (primary performance measure).
		\end{enumerate}
		
		All analyses will be conducted separately for expressions rated regarding their valence and the expressions rated regarding their arousal. \\
		
		Regarding aim (1), we will estimate the variance components of the following linear mixed model using the \texttt{gtheory} package \parencite{mooreGtheoryApplyGeneralizability2016} which uses the \verb|lmer()| function in R,
		
		\begin{itemize}
			\item \texttt{lmer(llm\_rating \textasciitilde  (1 | stimulus * model * format * example), ...)}
		\end{itemize}
		
		The ``stimulus'' term accounts for differences in the target construct between the expressions (i.e., actual differences in valence or arousal), whereas the remaining terms (``model'', ``format'', ``example'') account for methods effects (the facets in generalizability theory). If the mixed model cannot be fitted due to convergence issues, we will simplify the model structure by iteratively dropping higher-order interactions (first, the four-way interaction, then the three-way interactions not including the “stimulus” term and so on). 
		
		In line with generalizability theory, we will first perform a G study. We will fit the mixed-effects model above to estimate the variance components associated with each random intercept (main effects and interactions; \cite{brennanGeneralizabilityTheory2001}).
		
		In the D study, the universe score variance is defined as $\sigma^2(\tau)=\sigma^2(\text{stimulus})$ (i.e., as the random-intercept variance of items), because we aim to evaluate how well the obtained stimulus LLM ratings generalize across the investigated facets of the design (8 models, 4 example conditions, 2 response formats). The D study yields two types of coefficients. Our analysis will focus on the generalizability coefficient $E\rho^2$, which compares the universe score variance to the relative error variance $\sigma^2(\delta)$. The relative error consists of all components that include an interaction with the stimulus term, and therefore affect the ordering of stimulus ratings:
		
		\begin{equation} \label{eq.Erho}
			E\rho^2 = \frac{\sigma^2(\tau)}{\sigma^2(\tau) + \sigma^2(\delta)}
		\end{equation}
		
		Similar to consistency measures such as Cronbach’s $\alpha$, $E\rho^2$ ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating stronger agreement in the ordering of stimuli across facets. For completeness, we also report the dependability coefficient $\Phi$, which compares the universe score variance variance against the absolute error variance $\sigma^2(\Delta)$ and is defined as
		
		\begin{equation} \label{eq.Phi}
			\Phi = \frac{\sigma^2(\tau)}{\sigma^2(\tau) + \sigma^2(\Delta)}
		\end{equation}
		
		However, we do not consider the dependability coefficient to be relevant for the present investigation, as our study focuses on whether LLMs can produce stimulus ratings that show the same relative ordering (but not necessarily the exact same values) as those provided by humans.\\
	\end{examplebox}
	
	\begin{examplebox}
		Regarding aim (2) we will compute the correlation between human and LLM ratings of valence and arousal using the Pearson correlation coefficient,
		
		\begin{align}
			r_{xy} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})\cdot(y_i - \bar{y})}{(n-1) \cdot \text{SD}(x) \cdot \text{SD}(y)}.
		\end{align}
		
		We will compute the correlation at different levels of the analysis:
		
		\begin{itemize}
			\item (a) On the level of individual iterations in each simulation condition, we will compute the correlation between human ratings and LLM ratings across expressions. The inter-rater reliability (IRR) will be estimated by the average correlation:
			\begin{align}
				\widehat{IRR} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{sim}}}\hat{r}_i}{n_{\text{sim}}}.
			\end{align}
			To assess the estimation error due to the finite number of iterations, we will compute the Monte Carlo standard error:
			
			\begin{align}
				\text{MCSE}_{\widehat{IRR}} = \frac{\text{S}_{\widehat{IRR}}}{\sqrt{n_{\text{sim}}}}
			\end{align}
			where $\text{S}_{\widehat{\textit{IRR}}} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{sim}}}\{ \hat{r}_i - \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{sim}}} \hat{r}_i / n_{\text{sim}} \right) \} ^2 / (n_{\text{sim}} - 1)}$ is the sample standard deviation of the estimate and $n_{\text{sim}}$ is the number of simulation iterations (see \cite{siepeSimulationStudiesMethodological2024}).
			\item (b) On the level of aggregated LLM ratings (averaged across all simulation iterations in each simulation condition), we will compute the correlation between human and LLM ratings. Using a bootstrap procedure, $n_{\text{est}}$  LLM ratings (i.e., the number of ratings averaged per stimulus, e.g., 1--100) are first randomly drawn with replacement from the $n_{\text{sim}}$ iterations of a simulation condition for each stimulus and then aggregated into an average rating. These average ratings per stimulus will then be used to compute the inter-rater reliability between human-generated and LLM-generated ratings. This procedure is repeated $n_{\text{sim}}$ times to ensure that the same number of estimates is obtained as when using raw LLM ratings. 
		\end{itemize}
	\end{examplebox}

	\subsection{How do you plan on interpreting the performance measures? \textmd{(optional)}}
	
	\explanation{You can optionally specify thresholds for drawing conclusions to reduce post-hoc flexibility in interpreting the results (e.g., what are considered ‘acceptable’ and ‘unacceptable’ levels of performance and what are ‘relevant differences’ between LLMs in terms of their performance to fulfill a particular task). 
		Such thresholds are conventions rather than objective quality criteria; their main purpose is to commit to an interpretation before seeing the results, and they should be justified with respect to the intended use of the ratings. If specific comparisons between LLM configurations are planned (e.g., whether a smaller local model agrees with human ratings as well as a commercial model), specify them in advance together with the statistical test that will be used. Because both correlations share the same benchmark ratings, they are dependent and should be compared with tests for dependent correlations, for instance, as implemented in the R package \texttt{cocor} \parencite{diedenhofenCocorComprehensiveSolution2015}. In exploratory multiverse designs, the number of possible pairwise comparisons may be large, so that summarizing the overall pattern (e.g., by variance components or plots of all conditions) is often more useful than testing every pair. % [R1:17] 
	}
	
	\begin{examplebox}
		Regarding our primary performance measure, the correlation between human and LLM ratings, we define the following thresholds: an inter-rater reliability of $\widehat{\textit{IRR}} \geq .70$ will be labeled acceptable, $\widehat{\textit{IRR}} \geq .80$ adequate, and $\widehat{\textit{IRR}} \geq .90$ (as obtained with GPT-4o by \cite{martinezUsingLargeLanguage2024}) excellent. These cutoffs follow common conventions for reliability coefficients, which were proposed mainly in the context of test--retest reliability \parencite{grohUnreliabilityTestRetest2025, mathesonWeNeedTalk2019}. We consider a certain method as performing better (or worse) than a threshold on the employed corpus of text if the MCSE 95\% CI is above (below) this threshold. We consider method X as performing better than method Y on the employed corpus if the lower bound for the estimated inter-rater reliability of method X ($\widehat{IRR} - 1.96 \cdot \text{MCSE}$) is greater than the upper bound for method Y ($\widehat{IRR} + 1.96 \cdot \text{MCSE}$). Because the MCSE only reflects the finite number of iterations, these conclusions do not generalize to new stimuli. To draw conclusions about comparable stimulus material, we use the 95\% CI based on the averaged standard errors of the correlations between human-generated and LLM-generated ratings. If this interval is above (below) a threshold, we conclude that a method performs better (worse) than this threshold. 
	\end{examplebox}

	\begin{examplebox}
		Each LLM prompt consists of five components that are concatenated in each iteration of the simulation: 
		\begin{enumerate}
			\color{olive} \item An explanation of the task,
			\color{teal} \item the range of responses allowed, 
			\color{blue} \item examples for multi-word expressions, 
			\color{violet} \item the target multi-word expression to be rated, and
			\color{teal} \item a reminder for the range of responses allowed. 
		\end{enumerate}
		
		The examples below are based on Prompt 2 provided by Martínez et al. \parencite*{martinezUsingLargeLanguage2024} for ratings of valence. Ratings of arousal were analogous modifications of Prompt 3 (not provided here). \\
		
		\textbf{Example prompt with examples of low and high valence (original version of Prompt 2 in Martínez et al.): \\}
		\color{olive}Could you please rate how reading the following multi-word expression makes a person feel. \color{teal}Use a scale from 1 to 9, where 1 means very negative, bad and 9 means very positive, good. \color{blue}Examples of words that would get a rating of 1 are pedophile, AIDS, and wreck. Examples of words that would get a rating of 9 are vacation, fantastic, and laugh. \color{violet}The expression is: [insert expression here]. \color{teal} Only answer a number from 1 to 9. Please limit your answer to numbers. \\
		
		\color{black}\textbf{Example prompt with only examples of low valence: \\}
		\color{olive}Could you please rate how reading the following multi-word expression makes a person feel. \color{teal}Use a scale from 1 to 9, where 1 means very negative, bad and 9 means very positive, good. \color{blue}Examples of words that would get a rating of 1 are pedophile, AIDS, and wreck. \color{violet}The expression is: [insert expression here]. \color{teal}Only answer a number from 1 to 9. Please limit your answer to numbers. \\
		
		\color{black}Examples for multi-word expressions are (taken from \cite{martinezUsingLargeLanguage2024}):
		\begin{itemize}
			\item hit rock bottom
			\item fall in love
			\item ice cream cone
			\item drug addict
			\item starry sky
		\end{itemize}
